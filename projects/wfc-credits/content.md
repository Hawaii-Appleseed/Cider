[[ha-est-section-inner.h2-4]]
More Families in Poverty

[[ha-est-lead-in.span-4]]
LOSING THE EXPANSIONS PUSHES

[[ha-est-section-inner.p-3]]
These figures use the Census Bureau’s Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts tax credits and public benefits as income and adjusts for Hawaiʻi’s cost of living. Because the counts come from a survey sample, they are rounded to the nearest hundred and carry a wide margin of error, with a 90 percent range of 700 to 3,600 people and 0 to 1,000 children (Table 8). The estimate is static and does not count parents who leave work when the credit shrinks, which the model’s separate analysis of the state earned income tax credit finds would add to the total.

[[ha-est-section-inner.h2-5]]
How These Estimates Are Calculated

[[ha-est-lead-in.span-5]]
THE MODEL STARTS

[[ha-est-section-inner.p-4]]
These estimates answer one question, which is how much less working families would receive if the expansions end. To answer it, the model calculates each credit twice for the same households, once under the expanded rules and once under the earlier rules that return in 2028, and the difference is the loss, which is the part of each credit that Act 163 added (Table 2a). The model assumes that the share of eligible families who claim each credit stays where it is today, and it leaves out any other change a household might face.

[[ha-est-figcap.p-8]]
Table 5. Model Against Department of Taxation Records

[[ha-est-section-inner.p-5]]
The Department of Taxation’s report on the state earned income tax credit also breaks down 2024 claims by income and by filing status, which makes it possible to check who the model says receives the credit, not only how much. For incomes under $55,000, the model’s dollars are within 11 percent of the Department’s in each of the four income ranges, while for incomes of $55,000 and above the model has $1.4 million against the Department’s $0.8 million. By filing status, the model gives heads of household 51.5 percent of the dollars against the Department’s 55.5 percent, gives married couples more (41.8 percent against 28.9 percent) and gives single filers less (6.6 percent against 15.3 percent). Because the statewide total is set to match the Department’s, these differences only shift losses between family types and do not change the statewide figure.

[[ha-est-figcap.p-9]]
Table 6. State EITC by Income Range, 2024

[[ha-est-figcap.p-10]]
Table 7. State EITC by Filing Status, 2024

[[ha-est-source.p-5]]
The Department’s filing-status table reports the credit applied, which includes older nonrefundable credits carried forward, so it is compared in shares. The model counts Hawaiʻi residents; they are 94.6 percent of the Department’s claims and 97.3 percent of its dollars.

[[ha-est-section-inner.p-6]]
These estimates can be off for two reasons. The first is that they start from a survey of fewer than 6,000 Hawaiʻi households, and a different group of households would have given somewhat different numbers. Table 8 shows how much difference to expect, using a method the Census Bureau provides for this purpose, and the true figure should fall inside each range about nine times in ten if the survey sample were the only source of error. The ranges for the credit dollars are narrow because the model is set to match what the Department of Taxation actually paid, so the survey only affects how that total grows to 2028 and how it splits between the expanded and the earlier credits. The ranges for poverty are wide because they count only a few thousand people near the poverty line, and a small survey sample captures few of them.

[[ha-est-figcap.p-11]]
Table 8. Sampling Error of the Tax Year 2028 Estimates (90 Percent Confidence)

[[ha-est-section-inner.p-7]]
The second reason is that the model itself can be wrong, which a survey sample cannot measure. Table 5 checks it instead by comparing the model with the Department of Taxation’s records, and in years the model was not set to match, it came within 5 percent of the credit dollars the Department reported. Neither check covers the chance that incomes grow differently than the Congressional Budget Office projects, or that families respond to the change in ways the model does not capture.
