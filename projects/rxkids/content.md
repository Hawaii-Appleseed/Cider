[[what.h2]]
What is RxKeiki?

[[what.eyebrow]]
The Program

[[what.body]]
Based on the highly successful Rx Kids model, RxKeiki is a proposal to deliver **universal, no-strings-attached cash support** to every expecting mother in Hawaiʻi. There are Rx Kids programs in cities across Michigan.

[[what.scene.right.title]]
No Strings Attached

[[what.scene.right.body]]
Families decide how to use the funds based on their unique needs—whether for rent, food, baby supplies, or other essentials.

[[what.scene.left.title]]
Universal Support

[[what.scene.left.body]]
Every pregnant mother who lives in Hawaiʻi receives support—no income test, and no requirement to be enrolled in Medicaid or any other government programs.

[[what.scene.bottom.title]]
Critical First Year

[[what.scene.bottom.body]]
Monthly payments start at birth and run for six months (potentially up to twelve as funding allows), providing stability during the most important developmental period.

[[tanf.h2]]
How TANF helps pay for RxKeiki

[[tanf.intro]]
TANF (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families) is a federal program that gives each state a pot of money to help struggling families with children. Under the proposal, TANF can pay for part of RxKeiki, but only for families who qualify under Hawaiʻi's TANF plan. Every other family receives the same payments from state, county, and private funds.

[[tanf.compare1.title]]
TANF can give money in two different ways

[[tanf.ongoing.title]]
Ongoing aid

[[tanf.ongoing.tag]]
Comes with strings

[[tanf.ongoing.body]]
Monthly cash meant to cover a family's needs over the long term. To get it, families must follow rules—time limits, work requirements, and paperwork—and it can shrink other help like food assistance.

[[tanf.shortterm.title]]
Short-term help

[[tanf.shortterm.tag]]
No strings

[[tanf.shortterm.body]]
A brief payment for one specific moment—like welcoming a new baby. As long as it lasts no more than a few months, it counts as short-term help: no time limits, no work requirements, no strings attached.

[[tanf.split.title]]
How RxKeiki uses TANF

[[tanf.split.body]]
RxKeiki uses the no-strings, short-term TANF to cover the most important early stretch—the payment during pregnancy and the first three months after birth—for families who qualify. Everyone else gets the same payments from state, county, and private funds, so every family is treated alike.

[[tanf.choice.title]]
The choice that keeps it "no strings"

[[tanf.choice.summary]]
The proposed bill uses TANF for only the first four payments and pays everything else from state, county, and private funds. That keeps the whole program free of time limits and work rules.

[[tanf.more.button]]
How the bill keeps it no-strings

[[tanf.choice.body]]
There's a catch: TANF's no-strings help only lasts about four months. If TANF pays for more than that, the law treats it as ongoing aid—and the strings come back. (Limiting the program to families under an income cap does not change that—what matters is keeping TANF to those first few months.) That leaves Hawaiʻi two ways to build the program. The proposed bill takes the second:

[[tanf.option1.title]]
Use TANF for everything

[[tanf.option1.tag]]
Strings come back

[[tanf.option1.body]]
For families who qualify, TANF could pay for all 12 months. It's simpler to fund—but because the help now lasts longer than a few months, the law counts it as ongoing aid. That brings back time limits, work requirements, child-support rules, and smaller food and housing benefits.

[[tanf.option2.title]]
"Braided" funding

[[tanf.option2.tag]]
Stays no strings

[[tanf.option2.body]]
TANF pays only for the first few months, where it's truly no-strings. State and other funds cover the rest—plus the families who do not qualify for TANF. Everyone is covered, and the whole program stays no-strings. This is how Rx Kids already runs in Michigan, and how the proposed bill is written.

[[tanf.note]]
Last session's bill (HB2006) limited the program to families on Medicaid or under 300 percent of the poverty line, and it did not cap TANF to those first few months. The proposed 2027 bill fixes both: every family is eligible, and TANF can pay only for the prenatal payment and up to the first three monthly payments, for families who qualify (Medicaid can serve as the income test).[^rxkeiki-bill] It also pairs each government-funded payment with at least $1 of private money, paid out by a nonprofit administrator, to help protect SNAP and other benefits. State officials have warned about work rules, child support, and reduced benefits—those concerns are real under Option 1, but largely disappear under Option 2.

[[tanf.bottomline.title]]
The bottom line

[[tanf.bottomline.body]]
TANF pays for the start for families who qualify. Hawaiʻi pays for the rest. Every family gets the same help right when they need it most—with no strings attached.

[[bill.h2]]
What the proposed bill would do

[[bill.lead]]
RxKeiki would be a statewide program run through the Department of Human Services. This page summarizes the draft bill for the 2027 session in plain language. The bill is still a draft, and details may change before it is introduced.[^rxkeiki-bill]

[[bill.1.title]]
Who qualifies

[[bill.1.body]]
Every pregnant person who lives in Hawaiʻi and is at least 16 weeks along, and every household with a baby under one. There is no income or asset test, no need to be on Medicaid or any other benefit, and no TANF requirement. The state may only verify pregnancy or birth, identity, and residency.

[[bill.2.title]]
What families get

[[bill.2.body]]
$1,500 once during pregnancy, starting at 16 weeks. Then $500 a month from birth through the sixth month. Six more $500 payments run through the first birthday, as funding allows. Every family gets the same schedule, and the state may adjust the amounts for the cost of living.

[[bill.3.title]]
No strings attached

[[bill.3.body]]
Payments cannot depend on work, school, child-support cooperation, health care use, or how the money is spent.

[[bill.4.title]]
How TANF fits

[[bill.4.body]]
TANF can pay only for families who qualify under the state's TANF plan, and only for the pregnancy payment and up to the first three monthly payments. Medicaid can serve as the income test. Everyone else receives the same payments from state, county, or private funds. A payment is not ongoing TANF, so no time limits, work rules, or child-support requirements apply.

[[bill.5.title]]
Protecting other benefits

[[bill.5.body]]
Each payment that includes government money also includes at least $1 from a private donor and arrives as one blended payment from a nonprofit administrator. The state must work to keep the payments from counting as income for SNAP, Medicaid, child care assistance, and TANF, and every applicant gets a plain-language notice about their other benefits. The bill cannot guarantee every program will exclude the payment, and people on SSI are told to talk with a benefits counselor first.

[[bill.6.title]]
Who runs it, and how it's checked

[[bill.6.body]]
The Department of Human Services oversees the program and may contract a nonprofit administrator to enroll families, verify eligibility, issue payments, and guard against fraud, with community groups helping people sign up. Administration is capped at 5 percent of spending. Before each session, DHS reports on enrollment, who is reached, spending by source, administrative costs, private funds raised, and federal approvals.

[[bill.note]]
If enacted, the program would take effect July 1, 2027. Before the first payment, DHS must file the needed federal plan changes, get written federal guidance, sign an agreement with a nonprofit administrator, confirm the private funds, and publish its financing and benefits-protection plan. The draft leaves the appropriation amounts blank.
